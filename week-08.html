Architecture Briefs · Week 8

The Load-Bearing Habit

Every structure has walls that look decorative until you try to take one out. Habits work the same way. A family keeps a practice long after anyone can say why: a day that is not worked, a door that is not used, a thing that is always done before a meal. The reason was dropped somewhere along the line, but the habit kept carrying weight, because something in the structure still rested on it. That is what makes a habit useful to a reconstruction. Stories get edited every time they are retold, trimmed to fit whoever is listening; habits get copied without commentary, by hand and by body, so they are much harder to revise. When the explanation is missing and the practice survives anyway, you are looking at a beam the official version forgot to account for, still holding up a room nobody is allowed to name.

The official story calls these leftovers superstition, which is a way of filing them without reading them. Once a practice is labeled irrational, the inquiry ends before it starts. But a habit that outlives its explanation is not proof that the people who kept it were ignorant. It is proof that the explanation was removed and the structure was not.

What do you still do without being able to say why, and who stopped explaining it to you?

— Bjorn Aeolus
