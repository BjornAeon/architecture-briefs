Architecture Briefs · Week 7

The Overwritten Name

A name is the first layer of any structure, and the easiest one to replace. When a place, a role, or a practice is renamed, nothing has to be torn down; the old function keeps running under the new label, and within a generation the label is all anyone can see. The original name does not vanish. It drifts into dialect, into a family phrase, into a word that no longer fits the sentence it lives in. That misfit is the evidence, the spot where the new label slipped and the old shape showed through. A reconstruction reads those leftover words the way a surveyor reads a foundation line in the grass: not as decoration, but as the outline of what stood there first. You do not need the building to prove the building. You need the shape it left in the language.

The official story treats renaming as progress, a cleaner vocabulary for a better-understood world. But every rename also resets the start date. Once the new name is the only name, whatever came before it reads as prehistory, and prehistory is where inconvenient structures are sent so they never have to be answered. The work is not to argue with the new name. It is to hear the old one still sounding underneath it.

Which word do you still use that does not fit the story you were given about where it came from?

— Bjorn Aeolus
